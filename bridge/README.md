# Bridge

Play bridge with friends or robots in the browser. Up to four people play from their own phones or computers.

- **The page** is the files in this folder, served by GitHub Pages at `https://sundogx.github.io/bridge/`.
- **The tables** run on a Cloudflare Worker (`src/worker.js`, configured in `wrangler.jsonc`). Each table is a Durable Object that deals, checks every bid and play, runs the robots, and sends each player only their own cards, over a WebSocket. Nothing uses Claude's artifact storage.
- `config.js` holds the Worker's address (`window.BRIDGE_API`). Practice with robots works without it.

## Robot bidding system

The robots play **2/1 Game Forcing**, close to the ACBL 2/1 card. Conventional calls carry an alert that explains them. Every robot call can be tapped to read what it means (`describeCall` in `js/bidding.js` builds the text from the call's meaning in the system): calls the ACBL rules require to be alerted have a red outline, all other explained calls a gold one, and announced calls (a natural 1NT opening, transfers, a forcing 1NT) show their announcement. Robots explain their calls to everyone, their partner included; a human's own alert is hidden from that human's partner. `node tests/describe.js` checks the explanations. Robots read your calls by the system below whether you alert them or not, and calls the system doesn't use by their usual natural meaning (see *Partnering a human*).

- **Openings.** 1♥/1♠ five-card majors, 11-21 points (lighter in third seat, rule of 15 in fourth). 1♦ usually 4+ (3 only with 4-4-3-2), 1♣ 3+. 1NT 15-17 balanced (may hold a five-card major), 2NT 20-21. 2♣ strong and artificial (22+, or 9+ tricks); 2♦ waiting, 2NT rebid 22-24, 3♣ second negative over a major rebid. Weak twos in ♦/♥/♠ (6 cards, 5-10; 7 cards when the suit is too weak for the three level) with 2NT feature ask; three-level preempts with 7 cards (3♣ also on a good six-card suit when not vulnerable), 4♥/4♠ with 8.
- **Responding to 1♥/1♠.** 1NT forcing one round (6-12; a passed hand's 1NT is not forcing). A new suit at the two level is game forcing (2♥ over 1♠ shows 5). Single raise 6-10; 3 of the major is a limit raise with 4 trumps (a 3-card limit raise goes 1NT then 3 of the major); 4 of the major is preemptive. Jacoby 2NT: 4+ trumps, game forcing; opener shows a singleton or void at the three level, else 3 of the major = 17+, 3NT = 14-16, 4 of the major = minimum. Splinters: double jumps (1♥-3♠, 4♣, 4♦; 1♠-4♣, 4♦, 4♥) show 4+ trumps, game values and a singleton or void.
- **Responding to 1♣/1♦.** Majors up the line (a weak hand bids a 4-card major before diamonds); 1♦-2♣ is game forcing; raises 6-10 and limit; 1NT 6-10, 2NT 11-12, 3NT 13-15 without a 4-card major.
- **Opener's rebids.** 1NT 12-14, 2NT 18-19 (even with a 4-card spade suit over 1♥; responder bids 3♠ with four); reverses (16+) force one round; a **jump shift** (19+, or 17-18 with four losers, unbalanced) is game forcing; after 1M-1NT opener may rebid a 3-card minor. After 1x-1y-1NT, **new minor forcing** (2 of the unbid minor) asks for 3-card support or the other major; elsewhere the **fourth suit** is artificial and game forcing. Responder's new (third) suit after opener's rebid is forcing one round.
- **Notrump.** Stayman (Smolen over a 2♦ reply with 5-4 in the majors and game values; 2♥/2♠ over 2♦ = 5-4 invitational), Jacoby transfers with a super-accept, Texas 4♦/4♥, 2♠ = transfer to clubs for a weak minor (bid 3♦ to play diamonds), 2NT invitational, 4NT quantitative. The same over 2NT and 2♣-2♦-2NT one level higher, and over our 1NT overcall (15-18). If they overcall our 1NT at the two level, **lebensohl**: a new suit at two is to play, a three-level suit forces to game, double is penalty; 2NT asks opener for 3♣, after which responder passes or signs off in a suit ranking below theirs, invites in a suit ranking above, or shows game values with a stopper (their suit = 4 cards in an unbid major, 3NT = no major). Bid directly, the cue bid and 3NT deny a stopper ("fast denies").
- **Slams.** Hands are valued for slam beyond high cards: with a fit, losing-trick count (24 less both hands' losers, partner's losers estimated from what partner has shown) checked against points with shortness or length, less kings, queens and jacks opposite partner's shown shortness; partner is credited with a little above the bottom of the range shown. About 30 such points and 11 tricks, with no side suit that may hold two fast losers, start a slam try (the table-driven tries, such as Blackwood after a raise, a splinter or Jacoby 2NT, sit two points under the textbook counts); in notrump, about 31 combined high-card points invite with a quantitative 4NT (16 opposite 1NT, 11 opposite 2NT; after 1m-3NT opener bids 4NT on 17-18 and 6NT on 19+; after 1m-2NT 4NT on 19+). In a game-forcing auction, a **slow raise** (three of partner's suit when game was there to bid) agrees the suit and shows slam interest; partner then control-bids or signs off in game, and four of the major over a slow raise only says "no interest", so the slow raiser may still ask. Once a major is agreed in an auction that is not game forcing (1m-1M-2M, a limit raise…), a player with slam values but one side suit without a control makes a **control bid** in a new suit below four of the major, which makes the auction game forcing. **Control bids**: once a major is agreed in a game-forcing auction, a new suit below four of the major shows first- or second-round control (ace, king, singleton or void) and slam interest, cheapest first; with every side suit controlled between the hands, 4NT asks for key cards, and without interest the bid is four of the major. Roman Key Card Blackwood 1430 (5♣ 1 or 4, 5♦ 0 or 3, 5♥ 2 without the queen, 5♠ 2 with it) with the next step asking for the trump queen (with no queen, the asker stops at five); with all key cards and the queen, 5NT is a grand slam try (seven with two side kings, or one and five trumps). 4NT is quantitative when no suit is agreed and partner last bid notrump. After 2♣-2♦-3NT (25-27), responder bids 6NT with 8+, invites with 4NT on 6-7, or transfers with 4♦/4♥.
- **Game judgement.** The robots bid game with about 24 combined points (five of a minor with about 27) (their own valuation plus partner's expected strength), slightly more aggressive than 25, which suits IMP scoring. When a robot's system table has no call for its hand, it bids naturally rather than pass below game with values.
- **Competition.** Overcalls: 1-level 8-17 with 5 cards, 2-level 10-17 with a good suit; weak jump overcalls (6 cards at the two level, 7 at the three level, or a good six-card minor when not vulnerable); 1NT overcall 15-18 (11-15 in the balancing seat). Michaels cue bid (both majors over a minor; the other major and a minor over a major; advancer bids 2NT to ask for the minor) and Unusual 2NT (the two lowest unbid suits). Takeout doubles, with the advancer's cue bid showing 12+; jumps 9-12 opposite a direct double. A cue bid of their suit by partner of an overcaller (or by responder after an overcall) is a limit raise or better. Responder's doubles are **negative** through 3♠; opener reopens with a double when short in their suit. Over a takeout double: redouble 10+, Jordan 2NT = limit raise or better, jump raise preemptive. Partner's double of their 1NT is for penalties. Over their 1NT response the robots never overcall in a suit the opponents have bid. Later doubles are for penalties; competitive decisions follow the law of total tricks.
- **More competition.** The robots know the vulnerability. Not vulnerable against vulnerable, preempts, weak jump overcalls and one-level overcalls may be a point lighter; vulnerable, overcalls need about a point more or a better suit. Over their game the robots sacrifice with a long fit and little defence (9 trumps at the four level, 10 at the five level; 11 when both sides are vulnerable; never vulnerable against not vulnerable), and they double vulnerable opponents for penalty a point more readily. **Support doubles**: after 1x-1♥/1♠ and an overcall up to 2♥ (or a double), opener's double (redouble) shows exactly 3-card support; a raise shows 4. **Responsive doubles** also after partner's overcall and their raise (the two other suits, no fit); the overcaller then bids rather than defends. After partner doubles their weak two, **lebensohl**: 2NT is weak and asks for 3♣ (pass or sign off), a three-level suit shows 8-11, a cue bid or 3NT game values. Over their strong 2♣, suit overcalls are preemptive and double shows clubs; if they come in over our 2♣, responder's pass is waiting, a double is penalty, and our side is forced to game. When they redouble our takeout double, the advancer bids his best suit (pass = wants to defend: five good cards in their suit, or five of them and no five-card suit to run to), and so does the doubler if it comes back to him. Opener converts a negative double at the one level only with 5 good trumps.
- **After a takeout double or a redouble.** The doubler bids again over partner's simple advance only with extras: a raise shows 18+ in support points (19+ invites with a jump raise, game with 22+, or 18+ and three trumps when partner was pushed to the three level), 1NT/2NT 18-20 balanced, 3NT 22+ (20+ over a three-level advance), a new suit 18+ with five cards; all three points lighter after a balancing double or a free bid by partner. After 1x (X) XX, if they run below two of our suit every double is for penalty: opener doubles with four good trumps, else passes (forcing) unless he has a long suit or two-suiter to show; the redoubler then doubles with trumps, or bids (3NT with 14+ and a stopper, a raise, notrump with a stopper, his longest suit). If partner passes our opening and RHO comes in, opener's double is takeout with 16+.
- **Odds and ends.** A robot with a good six-card suit and about 13+ points (counting length) competes once at the three level. After 1x-1y-1z responder's jump to 2NT invites (11-12). After 1m-1M-3m (opener's six-card minor, 16-18) responder bids 3NT with 10+, or three of his major with five. Over their weak two, partner of a 2NT overcall bids game with 8+. In a contested auction, with a minor-suit fit, game values and their suits stopped, the robots prefer 3NT.
- **When they interfere in our auctions.** A double that takes no room (penalty or lead-directing, or any double once we are game forcing or opened 1NT, 2NT or 2♣) is ignored: the system stays on (completing transfers, answering Stayman, and so on). Once we are game forcing, a bid by them is ignored too when the call the system wants is still available; a double is then for penalty (trump length, or most of the high cards against their notrump, or their sacrifice over our game), and a penalty double is left in.
- **Partnering a human.** People make calls the robots never make. The robots read them by their usual natural meaning rather than as nonsense (`humanMean` in `js/bidding.js`), explain them that way, and never pass a forcing call below game. A responder's jump shift is strong (17+, five cards, game forcing) and a double jump preemptive; in competition a jump shift (or a jump raise of a minor, or of partner's overcall) is preemptive. 1♥/1♠-3NT is 13-15 balanced. Over our 1NT, three of a major is natural and game forcing (opener raises with three, else 3NT), three of a minor six cards inviting 3NT; over 2NT, 3♠ is natural. Opener's jump shift forces to game. After 1♥/1♠-2 of the major, a new suit (or 2NT) by opener is a help-suit game try: the robots bid game with 8 support points, or 6 and at most one loser in that suit. A bid in the opponents' suit is a strong forcing cue bid; 2NT over a takeout double of 1♣/1♦ is Jordan (a limit raise or better); 4♣ over 1NT or 2NT is Gerber, answered 4♦ 0 or 4 aces, 4♥ 1, 4♠ 2, 4NT 3 (4NT next is to play). `node tests/bidhuman.js` measures how robot partners cope with a human's natural deviations (see below).
- **Not played.** Inverted minors, Drury, help-suit game tries and Gerber (the robots answer both from a human partner but never use them), Bergen raises, serious 3NT, Last Train, Exclusion Blackwood, DONT/Cappelletti (Cappelletti was tried and measured slightly worse than natural overcalls and a penalty double), Leaping Michaels, Rosenkranz doubles. Inverted minors, Drury and help-suit game tries were tried and measured no better (see below).

Measured with `node tests/bidddsim.js` against the previous natural bidding (both systems bidding the same deals at two tables, contracts scored double-dummy) it gains about +1.0 IMP a board (3,000 held-out boards: +1.04 ± 0.09), and it lands nearer par.

The constructive changes above (slam valuation, control bids, the grand slam try and queen-ask fix, jump shifts, 2NT 18-19 rebids, responder's forcing new suit, no passing below game with values, 24-point games, 2♣-2♦-3NT responses) were tuned on 9,000 boards and measured on 4,000 held-out boards against the 2/1 system before them (`--base=basebid`): **+0.064 ± 0.025 IMPs a board**, with 170 boards bid differently (the gain comes from those). Inverted minor raises, Drury and help-suit game tries measured slightly negative (-0.001 to -0.004 a board on the tuning boards) and were left out; so was fast-arrival meaning for jumps to game.

The competitive changes above (vulnerability, support and responsive doubles, both lebensohls, sacrifices, defence to and after a strong 2♣, the system staying on over interference, running from a redouble, 24-point games in contested auctions too, and inviting 2NT only with 23+ combined) were tuned on 9,000 boards and measured on 4,000 held-out boards against the bidding with the constructive changes but none of these: **+0.27 ± 0.04 IMPs a board** (+0.38 ± 0.05 against `--base=basebid`). Against opponents who make a random call 15% of the time (as a human might), they gain about +0.28 IMPs a table. Cappelletti over their 1NT and sounder preempts and weak jump overcalls when vulnerable measured slightly worse and were left out. `node tests/bidddsim.js ... --off=supx,leb,...` switches features off by name to measure them.

The third round (the doubler's rebids, doubles after our redouble, opener's strong takeout double, competing with a six-card suit at the three level, the 2NT invitation and the replies to 1m-1M-3m, and game opposite a 2NT overcall) started from where `node tests/bidpar.js` and `node tests/bidloss.js` showed the IMPs going: in contested auctions mostly partscores where our side could make game, or where the side that bid on could have been doubled. It was tuned on 4,000 new boards and checked on four older 3,000-board sets, then measured once on 4,000 held-out boards against the bidding before it (`--base=basebid3`, `tests/fixtures/basebid3.js`): **+0.077 ± 0.020 IMPs a board**, with 226 boards bid differently, most of it in contested auctions (+0.41 ± 0.05 against `--base=basebid`). Each feature switches off with `--off=` (`xreb`, `xxrun`, `xstrong`, `ls3`, `j2n`, `j3m`, `o2n`), and `--k=name:value` sets the tuning knobs (`ls3`, `x3n`, `x3nl`, `xr1`, `o2n`). Bidding games and accepting invitations more lightly when vulnerable (IMP odds: a vulnerable game needs about 37%, a non-vulnerable one about 45%) measured no better, either way round, and was left out; so did a looser penalty double after our redouble (any three trumps) and a 3NT rebid by the doubler on 17-18. The 2NT-overcall change measured neutral and was kept because it is the standard treatment.

The fourth round started from `node tests/bidloss.js`: in uncontested auctions the biggest loss was game when slam makes (about 1,600 IMPs on 4,000 boards, plus about 800 where 6NT or six of a minor makes and the robots played four of a major or 3NT); with 30-32 combined high-card points a small slam makes double-dummy 70-80% of the time, and the robots bid one only about a third of the time. The changes (slam tries two points lighter everywhere, the slow raise and control bids above, the quantitative replies after 1m-3NT and 1m-2NT, five of a minor on 27 points) were tuned on the nine double-dummy sets of earlier rounds (32,000 boards) and measured once on 4,000 held-out boards against the bidding before them (`--base=basebid4`, `tests/fixtures/basebid4.js`): **+0.084 ± 0.026 IMPs a board**, 123 boards bid differently, almost all of it in uncontested slam and minor-suit game auctions (+0.12 ± 0.03 against `--base=basebid3`, +0.43 ± 0.05 against `--base=basebid`). Double-dummy scoring flatters slams a little (declarer always guesses right), so the slam settings stop short of the most aggressive ones that measured best: a natural slam target on 30 points and a quantitative 4NT on 15 opposite 1NT each measured about +0.004 more and were left out. Switches: `--off=slow`, `slowm`, `csign`, `ctl2`, `ctl2lo`, `q4`; knobs `--k=sp:` (slam points, 30), `st:` (slam tricks, 11), `sd:` (how much lighter the table tries are, 2), `n6:` (natural slam target, 31), `ntq:` (quantitative 4NT, combined 31), `nts:`, `g5m:` (minor game, 27), `g4:`, `g7:`, `g5:`, `ntf:`, `pef:`, `pec:`. Measured no better and left out: a lighter 4NT/6NT by responder after 1m-1x-1NT/2NT, lighter Blackwood after a 1NT major fit, earlier grand slam tries, a more optimistic estimate of partner's strength, and holding back Blackwood in a minor without enough key cards to stop at five (-0.002).

The fifth round was about robots partnering people. `node tests/bidhuman.js` lets one seat play "human": it bids like the robots except for one natural deviation a board, and its partner is either the robot before this round (`tests/fixtures/basebid5.js`) or the current one; every contract is scored double-dummy. On five sets of 4,000 boards (a human in each seat in turn, about 2,500 deviations a set), the everyday deviations (a four-card major opening, 1NT with a singleton or a good 14, a light opening, a weak two with five or seven cards, passing partner's opening with 6-7, an under- or overbid raise, a natural 2-over-1 on 10-12, 1NT over a minor hiding a four-card major, an overcall on a four-card suit, a takeout double with a long suit or off shape, a free bid on four cards instead of a negative double) cost the human's side between nothing and 1.6 IMPs each, and none of it was the robot misreading: the robot reads each call by the system, and its explanation says what the call shows in the system. What the robots did misread were calls the system does not use: they passed 1NT-3♥/3♠ (about -7 IMPs a time), read a weak jump shift in competition as 10+ points, a help-suit game try as a slam control bid, and a jump shift as not forcing; in all, the baseline robot passed 17 to 27 forcing calls a set. With the readings above the robot passes none, and gains about +6 to +8 IMPs per 1NT-3M deal, +1.4 to +2.3 per weak jump shift and about +0.5 per game try; jump shifts measure level (they are rare and the swings large). Over all the deviations together that is +0.07 to +0.11 IMPs a deviation on the four tuning sets and +0.03 ± 0.03 on the held-out set (most deviations need no help). The robots never make these calls themselves, so their own bidding is unchanged by them.

The round then tried, against `basebid5` on 32,000 double-dummy boards (two new sets and the six older ones), more competitive and preemptive changes: kept were the seven-card weak two, the six-card 3♣ preempt and three-level weak jump overcall in a minor (not vulnerable), and 3NT rather than a minor-suit fit in competition. Measured once on 4,000 held-out boards against the bidding before this round (`--base=basebid5`): **+0.015 ± 0.012 IMPs a board**, 64 boards bid differently (+0.051 ± 0.026 against `--base=basebid4`, +0.53 ± 0.05 against `--base=basebid`). Measured no better and left out: a takeout (not penalty) double when they overcall our 1NT at the two level, competing straight to the level of the fit by the law of total tricks, the overcaller's partner raising to game with five trumps on any strength, a five-card weak two in third seat, 4♥/4♠ on seven cards with a four-card side suit, sounder vulnerable three-level preempts, no minor preempt with a four-card major in first or second seat, and a three-level weak jump overcall on a six-card major. Switches: `--off=hum` (the natural readings of a human's calls), `humcue`, `gerber`, `nopassf` (never pass a forcing call), `mnt`, `w27`, `p6c`, `wj6`.

The sixth round was about people who overbid. The robots used to pass when a human jumped to 4♠ on a weak hand, overcalled at the three level on junk or bid 3NT with no stopper. Now, whenever a double of their contract is for penalty by the auction (a high-level double, a double of 3NT, a double after our side has bid, of a sacrifice), the robot estimates the defence: its own trump tricks and length, side-suit tricks and shortness, and partner's expected share of the points nobody has shown (each hand's shown range, scaled to the points it can't see, so a game the opponents' own calls don't support leaves partner more). The weights come from a linear fit to the double-dummy defence on about 15,000 such decisions (`judgeX`, `defTricks` in `js/bidding.js`). It doubles when the expected IMP gain, with their tricks spread around the estimate (standard deviation 1.5, 1 for slams) and scored doubled and undoubled at the vulnerability, is at least 3.5 IMPs, so a partscore that would make doubled into game needs a much better estimate than a game going two down. It also doubles instead of competing or sacrificing over their contract when the double gains 2 IMPs more than that (3.5 more in a game-forcing auction). Low-level takeout, negative, responsive and reopening doubles keep their meanings. Partner leaves a penalty double at the two level in as well, unless void in their suit with a six-card suit of its own or our side is in a game-forcing auction or has 25 points; and the advancer of a takeout double converts it to penalties (passes) with four or more of their trumps and two honours when the defence should beat it by a trick and a half (a trap pass). A judgement double is explained with the robot's reason, e.g. "Penalty: expects to beat 4♥ (about 2 down)" (the expected undertricks from its defence estimate; the text travels with the call as its alert).

`node tests/bidoverbid.js` measures it: one seat is a reckless human who bids like the robots except for one overbid a board (a jump to four of a six-card major or five of a six-card minor on a weak hand, a three-level overcall on 4-9 points, a light two-level overcall, 3NT with no stopper in their suit, a psyche, a raise straight to game on 4-9 points, bidding on over their game, competing one level higher). The triggers don't look at the other hands, so some of the overbids are right (the light overcalls and the psyches gain the human about as much as they lose). The human's partner bids like the baseline robots; the human's opponents are the baseline robots or the ones under test, and every contract is scored double-dummy. Measured once on 4,000 held-out boards (6,346 overbids) against the bidding before this round (`--base=basebid6`, `tests/fixtures/basebid6.js`): **+3.10 ± 0.08 IMPs per overbid** for the robots' side (from +0.8 on the light two-level overcalls to +6.6 on five of a minor). Robots against robots (`node tests/bidddsim.js --base=basebid6`) it gains as well, because the robots now double the baseline robots' own competitive bids and sacrifices when they go down: **+0.18 ± 0.04 IMPs a board** on the same held-out boards (+0.19 against `--base=basebid5`). Switches: `--off=pjx` (judgement doubles instead of passing), `pxb` (instead of bidding on), `pxf` (also in game-forcing auctions), `xpass2`, `xconv`; knobs `--k=pxm:` (the IMP bar, 3.5), `pxb:` (2), `pxf:` (1.5), `psd:` (spread, 1.5), `pxd:` (added to the estimate), `xcv:` (1.5). `--penlog=file` writes every decision with its features and the real double-dummy defence, for refitting the weights. Measured no worse but no better and left out: a higher bar for partscores that would be doubled into game, lower bars (0.7 to 2.5 IMPs gain the overbid set a little but lose against robots).

The seventh round tried two more changes and kept neither, so the robots' bidding is unchanged (`tests/fixtures/basebid7.js` is the bidding at its start). Against the robots' own contracts the defence estimate is about 0.3 tricks too high (0.15 in notrump), against the reckless human's about right; a correction for how the contract was reached (fitted on 4,000 boards: the declaring side's partner silent, a passed hand bidding, one player both bidding and declaring, a call the system makes) lost 0.03 IMPs per overbid on other boards, at the 3.5 and 3 IMP bars alike (`--k=fitx:1` switches it on). The features it uses do not tell a human's overbid from a robot's sound contract: in every bucket of every feature the gap stays about 0.35 tricks, so it comes from the hands nobody can see. Reading partner's takeout double of four of a suit as card-showing (the advancer passes with defence and no long suit, instead of bidding at the four or five level) changed 12 of 6,482 overbid deals (+0.004 ± 0.005 IMPs per overbid) and lost one 10-IMP board robots against robots on 4,000. `--penlog` rows now carry `hum` (the contract was bid by the human's side) and the consistency features, for the next refit.

The eighth round made the baseline fixtures load through `tests/fixtures/guard.js`, so `--off`, `--k` and `--penlog` apply only to the bidding under test (before, `--k=pxm:3` changed both sides and the fixture's rows mixed into the penalty log). A closer meaning for the penalty double in the auction (the robots' judgement doubles: 10th percentile of the doubler's points, 7+ in a suit and 4+ in notrump when partner has bid, no trump length shown, since half of them hold fewer than three trumps) measured -0.009 ± 0.005 IMPs per overbid on 4,000 held-out boards and was left out; the meaning stays 10+ points and 3+ trumps (`tests/fixtures/basebid8.js` is the bidding at the start of the round).

The ninth round (`tests/fixtures/basebid9.js` is the bidding at its start) looked for system holes in the worst contested boards of `node tests/bidpar.js`. Three changes were measured together: in competition, with about 22+ points between the two hands the robots competing one level above the law of total tricks (`lott22`, later taken out, see below); over their 1NT response a robot no longer overcalls in a suit the opponents have bid (the call stays in the table, so such an overcall by someone else is still read as natural, 7-15 with five cards); and when our takeout double is redoubled, the advancer passes with five cards in their suit and no five-card suit to run to. Against `--base=basebid9`: **+0.032 IMPs a board** on the four older 4,000-board sets used to pick them (r6a, r6b, r6hold, r7hold) and **+0.012 ± 0.009** on 7,000 fresh held-out boards (r9hold +0.008 ± 0.012, r9b +0.018 ± 0.014); against the reckless human of `bidoverbid.js` +0.04 to +0.07 IMPs per overbid. The extra competing costs a little when the robot partners a human who deviates (`bidhuman.js`: about -0.012 IMPs a deviation over six sets, from light openings and four-card majors that the robot credits with full values); `--off=lott22` switches it off. Measured and left out: lighter balancing over their two-level fit, raising a weak two to game with less, the slow (slam-interest) raise in game-forcing auctions on fewer extras (+0.02 on two sets, -0.02 on another), overcalling a good six-card suit instead of doubling (+0.011 on the tuning sets, -0.004 held out), and the doubler bidding a new major at the four level. `lott22` was then taken out: it cost about 0.01 IMPs a board when a robot partners a human (bidhuman; people open lighter than the robots expect), which matters more on this site than the small held-out gain. The other two stay (they fix −1400 disasters and measure flat held out).

## Online tables

How the server (`src/worker.js`) runs a table, beyond the rules themselves:

- **The host** (who opened the table) locks seats, seats people who ask, removes players, changes the settings and can **close the table** (`close`: everyone at it gets `notfound` with `closed: true`, the table and its lobby entry are deleted, and closing one room of a team match closes the other). The host can hand the job to a seated player (`makeHost`). It passes on by itself to a seated player who is connected when the host has been away for two minutes, or isn't at the table at all (not seated and not connected, as in the Closed room of a match the host opened); this happens with the next action at the table, and the chat says so.
- **Away players.** When a seated player's last connection closes they are marked away. Their partner can let a robot play for them at once, an opponent after half a minute (`sub`); the stand-in answers claims for them, and an undo request that needs their agreement lapses. They take over again as soon as they reconnect. If nobody does that and the table is waiting for them (their turn, or a claim or undo request that needs their answer), a robot stands in by itself after a minute, as long as someone else is still at the table. A seated player with no open connection counts as away even if the server never heard the connection close (as after a server restart). A stand-in never thinks in another player's browser (friends mode below): that would hand the away player's cards to someone else; the same holds when the robots finish a board whose time is up.
- **Moving seats.** Between boards the host can swap two seats (`swap` with two seats, people or robots or an empty seat) or move everyone one seat clockwise (`swap` with `'rotate'`); nobody moves mid-board. A new board isn't dealt while a seat is empty (a newcomer couldn't sit down until it ended), and a player the host removes mid-board is replaced by a robot so the board can finish. In the page, the host's end-of-board panel has a small **Seats** button (also Table settings, Move players) for both moves, and while a seat is empty the panel offers Add robot instead of Next board.
- **The session record.** Each finished board's record (`hist`) says who played each seat (`players`: name and id, or a robot; `sub` if a robot was standing in at the end), so totals can follow people who change seats. A board dealt again has `vs: { board, imps }`: N-S's IMPs against the N-S result of the board it repeats. The page's Results tab lists each person's IMPs against par over the boards they played (E-W's sign flipped), and a replayed board's panel and hand record say "Board 1 again: N-S +3 IMPs".
- **Time limits** (5 to 15 minutes a board): when time is up nobody can bid, play or claim, and the robots finish the board for everyone.
- **Playing a deal again.** After a board, the host can deal any finished board again (`next` with `{ replay: n }`), with its dealer and vulnerability, for example with the seats swapped. Its record says which board it repeats (`replayOf`). Not in a team match, whose deals are fixed.
- **The lobby** lists public tables active in the last 12 hours: the seats, the board, how many people are there now (`people`), whether the seats are locked, and the time limit. A table with nobody at it drops out once it has been idle for half an hour, and comes back when someone opens it. A public table where no board was ever dealt is deleted after a day. One address can open 20 tables an hour (counted in storage, so it holds while the lobby sleeps).
- **Limits.** About 10 actions a second and 5 chat lines in 10 seconds per person and per connection, three times that per address (friends at home share one); 12 connections per address per table, 60 onlookers.
- **Storage and hibernation.** A table sleeps between messages (hibernating WebSockets), so nothing that matters is kept only in memory: the table is in storage, and a friends-mode think request is kept on the connection it went to. Each move writes the table itself (a few KB); the chat (up to about 20 KB) is written only when a line is added and the finished boards (up to 100, about 170 KB) only when one is added. The alarm (robots' moves, the time limit, deleting a table after 3 days without play) is only rewritten when it has to change. When a table is full, connections that never said who they are make way after 20 seconds.
- **Tests.** `BRIDGE_URL=http://localhost:8787 node tests/online.test.mjs` plays through these flows with WebSocket clients against a running server (about four minutes). `tests/online-multi.test.mjs` (about two minutes) sends moves from several clients at the same moment (bids, cards, claims with undos, Next), and checks two tabs for one seat, reconnecting mid-trick, junk messages, rate limits, seat moves, the stand-in and the lobby. `tests/online-adv.test.mjs` (under a minute) tries what a hostile client could: oversized request bodies, sitting down after seeing hands, connection floods, a match onlooker reading table talk. Start the server with `npx wrangler dev --var TEST_TIME_SCALE:0.0167` (a minute lasts a second) to also check time limits and the host's away time.

## Claims

Any player except dummy may claim some of the remaining tricks (0 concedes them all), also in the middle of a trick. The claimer's cards are shown to the opponents who must answer (never to the claimer's partner), and the claimer's partner is never asked. A claim made against a stale count ("The number of tricks left changed; claim again"), for more tricks than are left, by dummy, while an undo request is open, or after the board's time is up is refused. A claim can be withdrawn until it is accepted; a rejection lets play continue.

**How robots answer.** A robot opponent (a table robot, or a stand-in for an away player online) answers at once. It accepts a claim of n tricks when the claimer's side takes at least n of the remaining tricks double dummy, judged on the actual cards: whatever the defence does, the claimer's side has a line that makes n. A concession of everything is always accepted. This is generous in one way: under Law 70 a claim that needs an unstated finesse is ruled against the claimer, and the double-dummy test accepts it when the finesse happens to work. Telling a stated line from a lucky one needs the claimer's statement, so the robots stay with the double-dummy rule. Human opponents still answer for themselves.

**The solver** (`claimSolver` in `js/bots.js`) is plain JavaScript, because the server can't run the WebAssembly solver within its CPU limit and the page's runs asynchronously in workers. It answers one yes/no question ("can this side take n?") by alpha-beta search over bitmask hands, tries one card of each run of equal cards, keeps a transposition table at the start of each trick keyed by who holds each card in relative rank order (with the lead that settled the position, tried first next time), and bounds every position by the sure tricks of the side on lead (its top winners, crossing once to partner's), by top trumps that can't be lost, and by suit lengths. It gives up after a node budget (about 0.4 µs a node): 20,000 nodes on the server, 300,000 on a practice table. Past the budget a robot accepts only claims within the old conservative count of sure winners (`claimTopTricks`).

**Robots claim** when the rest is clear: on lead at the start of a trick (and only after the first trick), a robot claims for its side the number of tricks its side takes double dummy, provided that number holds however the cards it can't see are split between the two hidden hands (respecting suits they have shown out of; adjacent unseen cards are moved as a group). So a robot never claims a finesse or a guess (a two-way guess between equal holdings can slip through). It claims everything, all but its sure losers, or concedes the rest. A defender robot whose partner is a person leaves claiming to them, a robot doesn't claim while time is up (the robots just finish), and after a claim of its side is rejected it waits two tricks before trying again. The claim appears in the chat ("West (robot) claims 4 of the remaining 5 tricks") and in the claim banner like a person's; robot opponents answer it at once. The same code (`robotClaim` in `js/table.js`, called from `A.robotStep`) runs on the server and in practice; deciding costs at most 6,000 nodes on the server (30,000 in the page), and only 500 with more than 8 tricks left.

**Checks.** `node tests/claimtest.js [n]` compares the solver with a brute-force minimax on n endings of 1-5 tricks and with the WebAssembly solver on n endings of 6-10 tricks (also mid-trick), times a claim answer on the server bundle, checks robots' claims against double dummy, and walks through the claim flows at a table. `node tests/claimsim.js [boards]` plays whole boards with four robots through the table actions and checks every robot claim against double dummy and against playing the board out.

Results (`claimtest.js 2000`, `claimsim.js 500`; CPU time on the server bundle in Node, least of three runs):

- No mismatches: 2,000 endings against brute force, 2,000 against the WebAssembly solver, and no robot answer or robot claim that disagrees with double dummy.
- One claim answer, 500 random claims near the right number at each length (half of them mid-trick): 6 tricks left p99 0.9 ms, 7 tricks 2.0 ms, 8 tricks 5.1 ms (max 6.1; 10% past the budget, then the conservative count), 9 tricks 6.5 ms (30% past the budget). Correct claims accepted: 280/280, 309/309, 285/313 and 237/324, against 17, 9, 10 and 7 with the old count.
- Robots deciding whether to claim: median 0.2 ms, p99 about 4 ms. In 500 robot-only boards 493 ended in a robot claim, most with 4 to 6 tricks left (2,449 tricks not played out), always with the double-dummy result from that point; playing on, the robots themselves would have got the same result 73% of the time (declarer one or more tricks fewer 18%, more 9%), since their heuristic play is not double dummy.

## Daily deals

Everyone gets the same 4 boards each UTC day (`js/daily.js`), dealt from the seed `daily:<date>` and played against the robots like practice. After each board the page posts it to `POST /api/daily`, and the leaderboard (`GET /api/daily/<date>`) shows each board's N-S scores with matchpoints and an overall ranking. Each day's results live in their own instance of the Lobby class (`daily:<date>`), deleted after 31 days.

**What the server checks** (`dailyCheck` in `src/worker.js`). The page sends the whole board, not a result: `{ date, board, secret, name, auction: ['P', '1S', ...], play: ['HK', ...], claim }`, with the calls from the dealer, the cards in the order played, and for a board that ended in a claim the number of the remaining tricks the claim gave declarer's side. The server deals the board itself from the seed and replays it with the same table actions the page and the online tables use (`A.call`, `A.card` from the bundled js/table.js): every call must be legal and by the seat whose turn it is, the auction must be over, every card must be a legal card from the right hand in turn. The contract and declarer come from that auction, the tricks from that play, and the score from those (`scoreNS`); nothing the page says about the contract, tricks or score is used. The robots' calls and cards are checked like anyone's. A board passed out has no play; a board played to the end has no claim; any other board must end in a claim. A claim may give N-S no more of the remaining tricks than they take double dummy from that point, whoever claimed: a claim by N-S is checked as a robot opponent checks one (`claimAccept` with the server's 20,000-node budget, about 8 ms; past the budget only claims within the sure winners `claimTopTricks` pass), and an E-W "concession" can't hand N-S tricks they couldn't win. Giving N-S fewer (a concession, or an E-W claim South accepted) only lowers the poster's own score, so it stands. A board turned round by "Rotate so I'm declarer" replays unchanged: the calls and cards are the same in the same order and N-S stays N-S. Pages from before this, which sent only `contract` and `declTricks`, get 400 "This page is out of date: reload it to post daily results".

**Claims the server can't check in time.** The server has about 10 ms of CPU a request on the free plan, so a claim with many tricks left in a complicated position may be beyond its budget. At a daily table the page runs the server's check itself (`Daily.serverTakes`, the same code and budget, so the same answer) and the robots turn down such a claim, and don't make one themselves, so play goes on and the claim can be made a trick or two later. (It wraps the page's `claimAnswer` and `robotClaimCount` for daily tables only.) A post the server refuses anyway shows a short message ("Board 2 was not added to the leaderboard: ...") and isn't sent again.

**Limits** (unchanged): one result per person per board, 40 accepted results an hour per address (a whole IPv6 /64 counts as one), 5,000 a board, 4,000 bytes a post. The deals can be computed by anyone from the seed, so the leaderboard can't stop someone who works out the best line beforehand, or who plays the robots' hands too; it does stop results that don't fit the deal.

**Tests.** `BRIDGE_URL=http://localhost:8787 node tests/daily.test.mjs` (run `node build.mjs` first): the 4 boards played by the shared robots (most end in a robot claim), posted as the page posts them and accepted with the server's score whatever score is sent; a board turned round; a passed-out board; a claim in the middle of a trick at exactly double dummy and a concession; then refusals: the old format, a fake score, 7NT redoubled claimed at trick one, illegal auctions (insufficient bid, doubling partner, a redouble without a double, an unfinished auction, calls after the end, an opening double), a passed-out board with play, cards out of order or played twice, a claim after the last card, a play cut short with no claim, a claim of more tricks than are left, an overclaim by one trick for N-S, an E-W concession of tricks N-S can't take, and a board's record posted as another board or another day.

## Limits

The free plan allows 100,000 Worker requests a day; 20 WebSocket messages count as one. A board uses about 80 messages. Tables unused for 3 days are deleted.

## Double-dummy analysis

The hand analysis runs DDS (Bo Haglund and Soren Hein, Apache-2.0) compiled to WebAssembly. The files are in `js/wasm/` with their licence notes.

- **The build.** `js/wasm/build.sh` (needs Emscripten: `brew install emscripten`) builds it from the same DDS 2.9.0 sources and C interface as the npm package `@bridge-tools/dd` 0.2.2 (MIT). That package's build is unoptimised, with assertions and a fixed 50 MB of memory per instance. This one is `-O3`, with memory that starts at 16 MB and grows. It solves about twice as fast, and the wasm is 304 KB instead of 705 KB, the glue 14 KB instead of 189 KB.
- **The workers.** `js/dds-worker.js` runs the solver in Web Workers. The page compiles the wasm once and sends it to each worker. Each worker **keeps one solver instance** for all its solves.
  - DDS keeps its transposition table between solves only for the same or a similar deal, as in its own batch calls.
  - An earlier note here said reuse gave wrong numbers. The cause was code that read stale entries from the reused output buffer, which is now cleared before every solve.
  - Creating and initialising an instance used to cost more than the solve itself in mid-play positions.
- **The checks.** `node tests/ddtest.js [n]` checks a reused instance against a brute-force search on n endings and on mid-trick positions card by card. It also checks full deals solved on a reused instance against a fresh instance. `FRESH=1` gives every solve a fresh instance instead. On 5,000 endings, 11,179 mid-trick cards and 400 full deals there were no mismatches.
- **Analysis workers.** The hand record uses one worker per strain job: two workers, or one on devices with four cores or fewer. It waits while a robot is thinking about a card or a lead.
- **Par.** `js/par.js` turns a table into the par contract.
- **Play review.** The hand record checks every card played (`playReview` in `js/analysis.js`): the position just before each card is solved for the player to move, and a card that takes fewer tricks for its side than the best card there is marked with what it cost (−1) in the play table, listed under it with the cards that were better, and named in the replay. It uses one worker of its own (made from a few lines in `js/analysis.js`, loading `wasm/compiled.js`, `engine.js` and `ddplay.js`), skips forced cards and runs of equals, and waits while a robot is thinking. A board takes about 0.1 s.

## Practice aids

- **Hints** (Options, off by default): in practice a Hint button (or H) shows what the robot would do in your seat: its call with the system's meaning, or its card (the solver's choice, as for the robots' own cards, else the heuristic's), outlined in gold until you act. Online tables have no hints.
- **Next board automatically** (Options, off by default): a practice board is followed by the next a few seconds after it ends, unless a window is open.
- **Kept on this device:** the practice table (its last 60 boards) is saved in the browser, so Leave table or a reload doesn't lose it. The home page then offers Continue practice and New practice.
- **Results** in practice: each board against a table of four robots playing the same deals, both scores and the IMPs, with the running total.
- Leaving an online table during a board you're seated at asks first, since it empties your seat.
- **Play from here** (`js/playfrom.js`): the hand record's card-by-card replay has a Play from here button, and each costly card in the play review has one too (it starts just before that card). It opens a practice table with the same hands, dealer, vulnerability and auction and the cards so far already played (put through the table's own actions, so the robots stay quiet), and you play on against the robots: from declarer's seat if your side declared (or you were watching), else from your own seat. The board is turned so that seat is South; a quarter turn moves the vulnerability with the seats, so the scores don't change. It works for practice, daily and online-table records, History records and shared or imported deals. The table is marked `whatIf` ("What if, board N" at the top): it is never saved over your practice table, and History/statistics, the daily leaderboard, Skip deal and Next board leave it alone. The end-of-board panel compares your result with the hand record's, in IMPs, and offers Back to the hand record (also at the top during play, which reopens the record at the same card; an online table is rejoined first) and Play again. Not while you're playing a board at an online table.
- **Just declare / Just defend** (Options, "Who bids in practice"): the robots bid all four hands, yours too, and you only play. Deals of the chosen practice deal type are tried (up to 0.4 s, about 11 ms a robots' auction) until the robots' contract is declared by your side (Just declare) or the opponents (Just defend); if none turns up the board is turned round so it is. When North declares, the board turns 180 degrees so you declare from South. Scored as usual: against par in the hand record and the robot table in Results. Undo before your first card is hidden (it would only reopen the robots' auction). Not for daily deals or deals played again; the robots' calls here are the usual system's even when BEN robots are on. `node tests/playfromtest.js` checks the turning and the robots' auctions.

## History: flags, notes, leaks; suit symbols in chat

- **Flags and notes** (`js/stats.js`, Options > History and statistics): the ☆ on a row flags the board; a saved board's hand record has the flag, a short note (kept with the record in IndexedDB) and a "‹ History" button back to the list. "Flagged" filters the list. Each row shows the contract and result and the opening lead; a note shows under its row.
- **PBN file / Print recap:** for the boards ticked after "Select", else every board the filters show. The PBN file is one `pbnBoard` per board (`js/analysis.js`), dated by the record, with the flag and note as a `{comment}`. The recap is a print-friendly page per board: diagram, auction, result and par, lead, note.
- **Where the IMPs went** (`js/leaks.js`): over the last 20/50/100/all of the filtered boards, the IMPs lost by kind. Contract kinds compare your contract, played double dummy, with par: doubled contracts, missed slam, missed game (par is your side's making game), overbid game or slam (your game or slam fails double dummy), part-score battles (no game either way), and other (strain, sacrifices). Play kinds come from the play review (`playReview`, read-only): the tricks your cards (and dummy's, when you declare) gave away double dummy, as declarer, on defence and with your opening lead, each turned into IMPs on that board's score. Each kind links to its boards. The double-dummy tables and reviews are worked out one board at a time only while the History window is open or no table is open, and the review result is stored in the record (`rv`), so a board is checked once.
- **Suit symbols in chat** (`js/chatsym.js`, Options, on by default): when you send a message, whole-word tokens become symbols: `2s` → 2♠, `4h` → 4♥, `3nt`/`3n` → 3NT, `4sx` → 4♠X, `sa` → ♠A, `dk` → ♦K, `h10` → ♥10. Pairs that are also words (`ha`, `da`, `ca`, `st`, `dj`, `sk`, `ct`, `hq`, `ht`, `sq`) are converted only in capitals, and links and addresses are left alone. The chat already draws ♥/♦ in red. Client-only: the server sees text.

## Bidding explorer and "What do you bid?"

Both are on the home page and in the table's More menu (`js/explorer.js`, `js/flashcards.js`; they only read `js/bidding.js`). Leave goes back to the table if one was open, else to the home page.

- **Bidding explorer.** Build any auction with the bidding box, with any dealer and vulnerability. Every call is listed with what the robots' system takes it to mean (`describeCall`: points, shape, forcing or invitational), marked Alert or Announced as at the table. Tap a call in the grid for the same text. Undo (or U / Backspace) and Clear. For the seat to call you can type a hand (`AKQ2 J54 T92 832`, spades first; dots, suit letters or symbols also work), deal one, or ask for an **Example hand**: one the robot would have bid exactly this way with (each of that seat's calls is its `botCall` there), else, after about a second, one that fits the points and suit lengths the calls show (`shown`). With a hand, the robot's call is shown with its meaning and a "Bid it" button. The auction, dealer and vulnerability are kept in `localStorage` (`bridge-ex`).
- **What do you bid?** Single decisions by topic: openings, responses (partner opened one of a suit, opponents silent), notrump auctions (our 1NT or 2NT opening, opponents silent), competition and slam (key-card asks and answers, control bids, the six and seven level), or mixed. A card is made by dealing and letting the robots bid until South faces a decision of that topic (N-S get the stronger hands for slam, notrump and responses; most passes are skipped). You see your hand and the auction, pick a call, and get the robot's call and its meaning, with yours. Only the robot's call counts as right: comparing the system's meanings was tried, but it reads too many different calls alike (2NT and 6NT, 3♠ and 4♠) to be a clear test. The streak, best streak and per-topic accuracy are kept in `localStorage` (`bridge-fc`). Keys: the bidding box (1-7, C D H S N, P, X), N for the next card.
- `node tests/flashcards.js [n]` makes n cards per topic and checks them (robot call, topic, South to call), the hand parser and the example hands.
## Lead practice

`js/leadtrainer.js`, from the home page or a practice table's More menu. The robots bid all four hands of random deals until East declares, so you (South) are on lead; the auction is shown with every call explained, and a menu picks any contract, games and slams, notrump or suit contracts. You tap a card. Then every card in your hand (runs of equal cards together) is rated on up to 200 layouts of the other three hands consistent with the auction, dealt and solved exactly as for the robots' own lead (`ddView`/`leadView` limits, `leadSample` in the page's DDS workers, up to 4 at once): the defenders' average tricks, how often the contract goes down, the cost in tricks against the best card, and the double-dummy result on the real deal. Your card is outlined and the best in bold; The deal shows all four hands. Next deals again (or N); Play it out carries on from your lead as a normal practice board (it replaces the saved practice table, like Play this deal).

A lead within 0.05 tricks of the best counts as the best lead; the streak, the share of best leads and the average cost are kept in localStorage (`bridge-lt`).

Speed: a 13-card lead solve is slow (median ~75 ms on one desktop core, with a tail of seconds), so the evaluation has a time budget, not just a count: each worker stops before a solve that would end after 11 s, and anything still running at 14.5 s is dropped. A desktop (4 workers) solves 200 layouts in 2-6 s; one desktop core alone manages ~145 in 11 s; a phone gets fewer layouts (roughly 50-150), never more than ~15 s. The note under the table gives the number of layouts and the time.

## MiniBridge, rubber and Chicago (practice)

Home page > **New practice** chooses the game and the scoring for a fresh practice table (`js/minibridge.js`, `js/rubber.js`; tests in `tests/minitest.js` and `tests/rubbertest.js`, run by `npm test`).

- **MiniBridge** (no bidding, for beginners): every player's high-card points are on the name plates. The side with more points declares, and of that side the player with more points. Ties: at 20-20 the dealer's side declares; partners with the same points, the one nearer the dealer (the dealer, or the first after the dealer clockwise). Dummy goes face up at once and declarer picks a contract from a small panel: a part-score (the 1-level contract in a strain) or game (5♣, 5♦, 4♥, 4♠, 3NT). The chosen contract is then played and scored like any other (so a part-score earns its tricks plus 50, a game its game bonus, and a failed game goes down from the game level); no doubles. Robot declarers choose the strain and target that scores best double dummy for them (the page's solver, used read-only), or guess from fit and points if the solver isn't available. Results compare each board with that double-dummy choice, in IMPs.
- **Rubber** and **Chicago** scoring: a score sheet (We = N-S, They = E-W) at the top of Results, above and below the line, with each game ruled off; the boards' dealer and vulnerability follow the rubber (a side with a game is vulnerable) or the four-deal Chicago cycle (none, dealer's side, dealer's side, both; dealers N, E, S, W; a passed-out deal is dealt again by the same dealer). Rubber: 700/500 rubber bonus, and an unfinished rubber's 300/100 shown as "if stopped now". Chicago: 300/500 for each game at once, 100 for a part-score on deal 4 that doesn't make game. Honours (four or five trump honours in one hand, or four aces at notrump) are an option. Duplicate scoring and the IMP comparison with the robots stay as they were alongside.
- Practice only. Online tables would need the log of boards and the dealer/vulnerability set-up in `js/table.js`'s `startBoard`/`finishBoard` (so the server runs it), MiniBridge's extra phase and declarer's choice as a server action (validated like a call), and the sheet sent to every seat.

### Learning mode and robot strength (`js/learn.js`)

## BEN robots (option)

**Use BEN robots** (Options, off by default, practice tables only) has the practice robots bid, lead and play with BEN (github.com/lorserker/ben, by Lorand Dali and Thorvald Aagaard, GPL-3.0). BEN's neural networks run in the browser in a dedicated worker, `ben/ben-worker.js`.

- **GPL boundary.** Everything BEN-derived is in `ben/`, under GPL-3.0. The app talks to it only through `postMessage`, with the messages in `ben/INTERFACE.md`. The page side, `js/benbridge.js`, is the app's own code and has no BEN code.
- **Turning it on.** Options shows the download size (from `ben/models/manifest.json`, else "about 60–100 MB") and asks first. A thin bar at the top of the page, and one in Options, shows the progress. The choice is kept in `localStorage` (`bridge-ben`). The option is hidden at online tables, where the server runs the robots.
- **Caching.** The worker keeps the models with the Cache API under `ben-models-v1`, so they download once. `sw.js` leaves `ben/models/` alone and deletes only its own `bridge-` caches.
- **Starting.** The worker starts on the first robot decision of a practice board, or when the option is turned on. Until it is ready the usual robots decide.
- **Fallback.** Each request waits about 4 s (5 s for a lead). A late answer, an error, or an illegal call or card means the usual robot decides that turn. A loading error, a worker crash, or two late answers in a row turn BEN off for the rest of the visit, with one toast.
- **Explanations.** BEN's calls are marked `ben: true` in the auction and get only the explanation the worker sends (`explain`, or an alert text), never the 2/1 system's text, since BEN plays another system. People's calls are explained as before.
- **Not covered by BEN:** the hint button's suggested call (it is still the usual robot's), and the comparison table in Results (four usual robots).
- **The stub.** Until the BEN port lands, `ben/ben-worker.js` answers with the app's own robots (`botCall`, `botLead`, `ddChoose`), so the option works end to end.

## Robot card play

In practice games the robots choose their cards the way GIB and BEN do, by double-dummy sampling (`js/ddplay.js`). On a robot's turn the page sends a worker only what that robot may see: its own hand, dummy once it is face up (declarer sees both of its hands), the cards played so far, and the auction. The worker deals the unseen cards at random about 24 times, consistent with that: suits a player has shown out of, and what the auction showed (`ddAuctionLimits`: the minimum and maximum points and suit lengths and a balanced hand, from `shown()` and the call meanings in `js/bidding.js` with the board's vulnerability, plus the opening-bid ranges). The limits are soft: a layout outside them is used only when too few fit, and after the lead it is weighted down by 0.7 for each point it misses by, and 0.7² for each card of suit length (or for a singleton in a hand that showed a balanced one). It solves every layout for every legal card and plays the card with the best average score for its side. The heuristic robot (`botPlay`) breaks ties, which keeps sequence leads, signals and low-from-equals. It also plays outright when there is only one sensible card, when the solver takes more than about 1.5 seconds, or if the solver fails. The solver stops before a layout that would probably take it past the time budget, using the average time per solve so far. A late answer only means the heuristic card for that turn: the worker isn't restarted unless it is more than 10 seconds behind. The solver thinks during the robot's usual pause, so play is no slower than before. In practice games the workers are started and warmed up during the auction (`robotDDWarm`), so the opening lead and the first cards don't wait for them.

### Opening leads

The opening lead is the decision with the least information, so it has its own code, `js/leads.js`.

- **The card in a suit** is the standard one: top of a sequence (KQJ, QJT, and KQT), the middle card of an interior sequence (K**J**T, Q**T**9, and A**J**T against notrump), the ace against a suit contract (no underleading aces), top of a doubleton, fourth best from an honour, and second highest from three or four small cards.
- **The prior** rates leading each suit from the leader's holding and the auction, the way Bird and Anthias's *Winning Notrump Leads* and *Winning Suit Contract Leads* rate leads by simulation. It is a linear score on 25 features: sequences, AK, interior sequences, length, singletons and doubletons, unsupported kings, queens and aces, honour doubletons, partner's suit, the opponents' suits, unbid suits, majors against notrump, trumps, and trumps after dummy gave preference. The weights are fitted (ridge regression) to the double-dummy result of every lead on 1,500 robot auctions: `node tests/leadsim.js fit`. The fit agrees with the books:
  - Sequences, AK and partner's suit are good leads.
  - Unsupported kings and queens, and the opponents' suits, are bad leads.
  - Against suit contracts, singletons and doubletons are good, and a trump lead is slightly bad.
  - Against notrump, length and the majors help.
- **The heuristic robot** (`botLead`, used in strangers mode and as the fallback) leads the suit with the best prior.
- **The solver robot** (`leadChoose`, `leadPick`) deals the unseen cards up to 48 times, consistent with the auction (`leadLimits`). It solves each layout and leads the suit whose card takes the most defensive tricks on average, plus twice the prior.
  - `leadLimits` uses the minimum *and* maximum HCP and suit lengths from `shown()`, and no five-card major for a direct 1NT-3NT.
  - The prior steadies the choice, because ~24-48 layouts are a noisy estimate. Averaging IMPs or points instead of tricks measured about the same.
  - The page splits the layouts between workers: half the device's cores, from one to four (`robotDDLead` in `js/analysis.js`). Each worker sends back every result as soon as it has solved it, and stops before a layout that would probably end past the 1.7-second budget. The page pools what has arrived and decides by about 2.2 seconds at the latest, so a slow worker still contributes. The lead workers also share the first two tricks' card decisions (below), are closed after that, and are started again during the next auction.
  - `ddView` adds the limits and the prior to the robot's view (`leadView`), so in friends mode the browser doing the thinking needs nothing more.

Measured with `node tests/leadsim.js` on 2,215 held-out boards. Each board is a robot auction, and every lead is scored by the true double-dummy result, with 24 layouts. The comparison is against the previously shipped rule: the old heuristic lead, replaced only by another suit's card that the solver rated 30 points better on 24 layouts.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Heuristic `botLead` (new) | +0.01 ± 0.04 | +0.02 | 49.8% | +0.11 ± 0.08 |

### The heuristic robot (`botPlay`)

`botPlay` in `js/bots.js` plays without the solver. It is what the robots use at strangers tables and on the server, the fallback when the solver is slow, the tie-breaker inside the solver play, and the play model that `ddConsistency` replays. It is synchronous and cheap (about 0.035 ms a card on average, 0.016 ms at the median and under 0.1 ms at p99; the opening lead is `botLead`), and it reads only what that seat may see.

- **Rules first, then a fitted choice.** Each kind of decision has hand-written rules that choose a card. For the declaring side's leads, for following suit in second and third seat, and for the defenders' switches and discards, a linear score then rates a few candidate cards and may override the rule. One of the features is "this is the rule's card", so the fit only corrects the rules where they go wrong.
- **Declarer counts and plans** (`suitCount`, `declPlan`, `declRule`). For each suit it counts the sure tricks from the top, and the tricks once the suit is developed (round by round: our top card wins if it beats theirs, otherwise we duck and they win).
  - In a suit contract it ruffs losers in the short trump hand first. It draws trumps with a master, toward partner's higher trumps, or with the top of a sequence. It leaves the opponents' trumps alone when they are all higher than ours (also when it has run out of other plans). When the short trump hand has a singleton in a side suit with losers opposite, it cashes the master first to make the void (`mkvoid`).
  - If the sure tricks are enough, it cashes them, short hand first: when partner is shorter in the suit and holds a master, it leads low to it rather than cashing its own (`cashIn`, also for trumps). If not, it develops the suit that gains the most tricks (leading toward honours, or the top of a sequence) instead of cashing winners early.
  - The fitted suit choice (`dlF`, `BOT_DLEAD_W`) weighs, among others: ruffs in the other hand and crossruffs, the opponents' known voids, sure and developable tricks, a tenace opposite, getting to partner's winners, side suits the opponents may ruff, and whether the card led would give a trick away.
- **Declarer's third hand counts the cards.** It finesses only against one missing card, and takes the master when the sure tricks are already enough. It also reads the auction: from what each defender's calls showed (`seatShown`, the meanings `auctionMetas` in `js/bidding.js` gives, once per auction; a hand that passed when it could open has at most 11) and the high cards each has played, it estimates the second hand's share of the opponents' remaining high cards (`oppShare`), and doesn't finesse when that share is under 0.45 (`finAuc`). If the second hand showed out, the fourth hand holds everything, so it plays the cheapest card that beats it all. If the fourth hand is void, it plays low when that hand will ruff, else the cheapest winner. This alone gained about +0.6 IMPs a board.
- **Following suit** (`flF`, `BOT_FOLLOW_W`, by side, seat and strain): the candidates are the rule's card, low, the cheapest winner, the cheapest sure winner and high (equal cards count once, as the lowest). The features include whether the card wins now or for sure (dummy is visible, voids are known), how many unseen cards beat it, covering an honour, splitting honours in second seat, unblocking, a finesse against one card when the opponents have few or many cards left in the suit, and whether winning now beats the contract. The fit is used in second seat for both sides and in third seat for the defenders. In the other seats it did not beat the rules. When the declaring side plays second and the fourth hand (partner, visible) holds a master that beats the card led, it plays low and lets that master win, instead of taking the trick with its own (`p2low`, round 7; the fit is skipped there). Before, dummy took the K from Kx opposite a singleton A, or the Q from AQxx opposite Kxx.
- **Defenders** choose a suit to lead after the opening lead (`swF`) and a suit to discard (`dsF`) by a linear score on simple features. These include partner's signals and lead, leading through strength or up to weakness, ruffs for dummy or ruff-and-discard, honours that would be led away from or left unguarded, length against dummy's, and the suits the opponents bid. They also include an ace without the king, two cashing winners, a passive trump lead, declarer's known voids, a tenace in dummy that we would lead into, and dummy's long running suit. In second seat a defender takes a master when it and our other sure winners beat the contract (`set2`). A defender void in second seat doesn't ruff low in front of a dummy that will ruff too (it ruffs above dummy's trumps or discards), nor when the trick may still be partner's (`r2over`, `r2part`, and `r2dm` when dummy led and we hold a trump honour). The rest is standard: second hand low, third hand high, covering an honour with an honour, cashing the setting tricks, and attitude signals. The setting tricks are cashed at once only when the sure winners are exactly enough and the contract isn't doubled: in a doubled contract the defenders play for the biggest penalty, and cashing early mostly sets up declarer's cards. Against a crossruff (declarer has shown out of a side suit, dummy has trumps and is short in a suit we hold) the switch leans to a trump lead (`trX`).
- **The fits** are ridge regressions on every candidate of each decision in robot games: `node tests/heurfit.js [boards] switch|discard|lead|follow [seed]`. `Y=dd` scores each candidate by its double-dummy result. `Y=roll` plays the rest of the deal out with the heuristic in all four seats. `Y=mix`, used for all the weights now, averages the two.
  - Double dummy alone undervalues safe plays against imperfect opponents. Rollouts alone would tune the robots to beat their own heuristic.
  - Refitting after the play changes helped once (declarer's leads, +0.16 IMPs). After that it was flat, except in round 3: the discard weights (until then fitted to double dummy only) refitted with `Y=mix`, and the switch weights refitted on 6,000 boards (seed `fit4`).
- **Measured and left out** (neutral or worse on the tuning seeds): a hold-up with a single stopper in notrump by the rule of 7 (`holdup`, also as a feature in the following fit), leading trumps against dummy's ruffs (`trlead`), a trump finesse toward partner's tenace with eight trumps or fewer, reading every discard by partner as discouraging, and a fitted choice between ruffing low, ruffing high and discarding. Letting partner's honour run in third seat when we hold the next card measured −0.03 and was removed. Round 3 also left out: the defenders' switch with features from declarer's points and lengths shown in the auction (partner's likely share of the honours in the suit), discard features from the opponents' shown lengths, a second candidate card in each suit for declarer's leads (with card features for entries and unblocking), a conditional-logit fit instead of ridge (better held-out pick rates in the fit, no gain in play), splitting the switch weights by context, a deeper finesse when the second hand has most of the high cards, the vacant-places odds for the finesse, playing for the drop with two or three cards out, and refitting declarer's leads on 6,000 boards. Using the exact claim solver for endgames was skipped: declarer's choices with ten or fewer layouts of the defenders' cards left lose only about 0.06 tricks a board, and solving each would cost far more than the time per card allows.

- **Faster play check (shipped).** From trick 5 on, checking 96 layouts against the play (170-480 ms in Node under load) costs far more than solving 24 of them (13-130 ms), and it grows with every card played. Three shortcuts now leave the result exactly as it was: a layout dealt more than once (common late in the play) is checked and solved once (`memo`); a card choice is not replayed through the heuristic when all of the player's legal cards were equal (`skipEq`); and a layout's check stops as soon as its weight falls below the 24th best so far, since it can then no longer be among the layouts solved (`floor`; ties already went to the earlier layout). Each has an option to switch it off for comparisons. They choose the same layouts with the same weights in 415 of 415 decisions over 12 boards (and `tests/ddtest.js` checks this), with 37% of the heuristic calls: 86% at trick 1, 77% at trick 4, 59% at trick 7, 40% at trick 8, 26% at trick 9 and 1-11% at tricks 10-12. (`memo` and `skipEq` together 42%, `floor` alone 90%.) With all three, `ddacc` changed 0 of 876 decisions (80 boards, every third card from the first trick on); with `memo` alone, 0 of 1,898 (100 boards from trick 6 on). On the phone proxy (budgets cut to a quarter, one thread, 10 boards, 357 decisions, on a heavily loaded Mac), tricks 7-13 took 24 ms of CPU a decision on average with the shortcuts instead of 42 (90th percentile 51 instead of 71; `memo` alone 22 and 42), and the median wall time was 177 ms instead of 215. How often the solver ran out of wall-clock time there (64 and 56 of 357) depended on the other programs' load more than on the shortcuts. The first tricks are dominated by the solves and barely change. On a slow phone the check is cut off at 30% of the budget (`consMs`), so a faster check also means more of the 96 layouts are checked there.

**Time.** In Node a solve takes 50-85 ms at trick 1 (all four hands nearly full), about 10 ms at trick 3 and under 1.5 ms from trick 5; the play check costs about 75 ms for 96 layouts. The first two tricks are therefore split between the lead's workers (half the cores, two to four), which stay open until trick 3: each deals and checks its share of the pool and solves its share of the layouts, and the page pools the results and their weights (`robotDDSplit`, `ddDecide`). The play check stops at 30% of the budget (`consMs`), and a decision gives up early when it can't solve 8 layouts before the page stops waiting (`hardMs`), so a slow worker isn't left busy into the next card. On a simulated phone four times slower than this Mac (`performance.now` scaled), 290 decisions over 8 boards: median 125 ms, 90th percentile 240 ms with four workers (520 ms with one), slowest 1.5 s; trick 1 averages 330 ms with four workers, 510 ms with two and 740 ms with one. A DDS build with the large transposition table (`SetResources(160, 1)`) solved trick 1 only 6% faster (trick 3 30%) and isn't used.

BEN itself (github.com/lorserker/ben) is GPL-3.0 and its card-play networks are about 7 MB each (eight of them, plus 11 MB for onnxruntime-web), trained on BEN's own bidding, so they aren't used here; `tests/notes-ben-leads.md` has notes on its opening-lead model.

Online tables have a host setting, **Friends mode**, for private tables only (on by default there; a public table never has it). A Durable Object on the Workers Free plan gets about 10 ms of CPU time per invocation, and one solve of a full deal takes about 70 ms, so the server can't run the solver itself. In friends mode it sends the robot's view (`ddView`) to one connected player's browser (seated players first, in turn; onlookers only if nobody is seated, and never in a team match), which solves it and sends the card back (`think` and `thought` messages in `src/worker.js` and `js/net.js`; the request carries a random nonce, and only the browser asked may answer). That browser then holds the robot's cards, which is why it is meant for friends. If no answer arrives within 2.5 seconds the robot plays its heuristic card, and a browser that has let two requests in a row run out (a phone asleep) is asked only one time in eight after that. With friends mode off (strangers), no hidden card ever leaves the server and the robots play the heuristic.
